From Industry to PhD: Why I Made the Switch

📅 December 2025 ⏱️ 6 min read 🏷️ Career, Academia, Personal

In August 2023, I walked away from a comfortable job as a Lead Data Scientist at EY—managing teams, working with Fortune 500 clients, earning a good salary—to become a first-year PhD student. Everyone thought I was crazy. Here's why I did it and what I've learned so far.

The Comfortable Trap

Let me be clear: I loved my job at EY. I was leading projects, mentoring junior data scientists, and solving real business problems. The work was impactful, the people were great, and I was learning a lot. But somewhere around year three, I noticed something.

I was getting really good at building the same kinds of ML systems over and over. Each project felt like a variation on a theme. The technical challenges were interesting but predictable. I could see exactly what my career would look like in 5, 10, 15 years.

"Comfortable isn't always bad. But when comfortable starts feeling like complacent, it's time to pay attention."

What Changed

Two things happened that made me seriously reconsider:

1. The Questions Got More Interesting

In industry, I was answering questions like "How can we predict X to optimize Y?" Important questions, sure. But in my free time, I was obsessing over questions like "Why do language models fail at compositional reasoning?" and "How can we build evaluation metrics that actually measure understanding?"

The questions I found myself drawn to weren't the ones I was being paid to answer.

2. The LLM Moment

When GPT-3 came out, then GPT-4, I realized we were at an inflection point. The field was moving incredibly fast, and the most interesting work was happening in research labs. I didn't want to watch from the sidelines.

The Decision

Deciding to leave wasn't easy. Here's what I weighed:

What I'd Be Giving Up

  • Financial stability and career progression
  • A senior position with real impact
  • Mentorship opportunities with my team
  • The satisfaction of shipping production systems

What I'd Be Gaining

  • Time to pursue fundamental research questions
  • Freedom to explore ideas without immediate ROI concerns
  • Deep expertise in cutting-edge ML techniques
  • A community of people asking similar questions

The tipping point was realizing that if I didn't try now, I'd always wonder. I could always go back to industry (hopefully with better skills), but the window for starting a PhD felt finite.

What I've Learned (So Far)

Industry Experience Was Invaluable

I'm so glad I didn't go straight from undergrad to PhD. Four years in industry taught me:

  • How to scope problems and actually finish projects
  • What makes research useful vs. academically interesting
  • How to work with non-technical stakeholders (great for explaining research)
  • That perfect is the enemy of done

PhDs Are Different Than I Expected

The hardest parts aren't what I thought they'd be:

  • Easiest: The technical work. Industry prepared me well for this.
  • Hardest: The ambiguity. No one tells you what to work on. You have to figure it out.
  • Most surprising: How collaborative research is. I thought it would be lonely; it's not.

The Tradeoffs Are Real

I won't sugarcoat it—there are costs:

  • PhD stipend vs. industry salary is... a significant drop
  • Progress is slower and less tangible than shipping features
  • Imposter syndrome hits different when surrounded by brilliant researchers
  • Work-life balance is on you to define (no one enforces it)

Would I Do It Again?

Absolutely. But with caveats. A PhD isn't for everyone, and it's not the only path to doing impactful work. It's right for me because:

  1. I genuinely enjoy the research process, even when it's frustrating
  2. The questions I care about require deep, sustained investigation
  3. I have the financial flexibility to make it work
  4. I found an advisor whose research aligns with my interests

Advice If You're Considering the Switch

  • Get research experience first - Do internships, side projects, or collaborations before committing
  • Be honest about your motivations - If you just want to learn, there are other ways
  • Talk to current PhD students - Ask about the hard parts, not just the exciting parts
  • Consider your financial situation - Can you afford the pay cut for 4-6 years?
  • Think about alternatives - Research labs, residencies, and industry research roles exist

Final Thoughts

The best career decisions aren't always the most logical ones. Sometimes you have to make the choice that feels right, even if you can't fully justify it on a spreadsheet.

For me, coming to do a PhD wasn't about titles or prestige. It was about giving myself permission to work on problems I find deeply fascinating, even if they don't have immediate commercial value.

A year and a half in, I don't regret it. Ask me again in three years. 😄
