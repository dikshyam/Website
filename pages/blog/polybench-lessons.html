Building PolyBench: Lessons in Benchmark Design

📅 January 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read 🏷️ Research, Benchmarking, LLMs

Creating a good benchmark is harder than it looks. When we started building PolyBench—a 125K+ task benchmark for evaluating LLMs on polymer design—I thought the hard part would be collecting data. Turns out, the real challenge is asking the right questions.

The Problem with Most Benchmarks

Most LLM benchmarks test memorization, not reasoning. They're essentially fancy multiple-choice tests where models can score well by pattern matching without truly understanding the underlying concepts. This is especially problematic in scientific domains where we need models that can actually reason about complex, multi-step problems.

What We Wanted to Build

We had three main goals for PolyBench:

  • Test compositional reasoning - Can models break down complex problems into steps?
  • Provide diagnostic insights - Where exactly do models fail?
  • Enable knowledge augmentation - Can we help models learn to use external knowledge effectively?

Design Decisions That Mattered

1. Multi-Constraint Tasks

Instead of simple Q&A, we created tasks with multiple constraints that need to be satisfied simultaneously. For example: "Design a polymer with high tensile strength, low cost, and biodegradability." This forces models to reason about tradeoffs, not just recall facts.

2. Reasoning Traces

We didn't just want right answers—we wanted to see how models arrive at those answers. So we created knowledge-augmented reasoning traces that show the step-by-step thought process. This lets us diagnose where reasoning breaks down.

"A benchmark that only tells you the final score is like a compiler that only says 'error' without pointing to the line number."

3. Diagnostic Evaluation

The most important innovation was making the benchmark diagnostic. We can now answer questions like:

  • Does the model struggle with constraint combination?
  • Is it failing at knowledge retrieval or reasoning?
  • Which specific polymer properties are hardest to reason about?

What We Learned

Scale Matters, But Not How You Think

125K tasks sounds like a lot, but what really matters is diversity. We spent more time ensuring coverage of different reasoning patterns than just generating more examples of the same pattern.

Ground Truth is Hard

In scientific domains, getting ground truth is challenging. We had to work closely with domain experts and validate against existing literature. This was time-consuming but absolutely necessary.

Evaluation ≠ Training

A good benchmark should evaluate capabilities, not be another training dataset. We designed PolyBench to be difficult to game—you can't just memorize your way to a good score.

Practical Takeaways

If you're building a benchmark (or evaluating models on existing ones), here's what I'd recommend:

  1. Start with the failure modes - What do you want to catch? Design for that.
  2. Make it diagnostic - Aggregate scores hide information. Show where models fail.
  3. Test reasoning, not recall - Multi-step problems with constraints force real thinking.
  4. Involve domain experts - You can't evaluate scientific reasoning without scientists.
  5. Iterate with real models - Run models on your benchmark early and often. You'll find issues.

Looking Forward

PolyBench is just the beginning. We're working on extending these ideas to other scientific domains and exploring how to make evaluation even more insightful. The goal isn't just to rank models—it's to understand them well enough to make them better.

If you're interested in the technical details, check out our paper on arXiv: Teaching and Evaluating LLMs to Reason About Polymer Design Related Tasks
